## Part A: Dataset and Graph Exploration

**GQ1.** Freland has the largest degree at 5, since it trades with Borland, Cyrenia, Deltora, Galenia, and Hesperia. Estovia has the smallest at 2, with only Deltora and Hesperia. We got the degrees by summing each row of A_full (`A_full.sum(dim=1)`), which gave `[3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 5, 4, 3]`, so the mean degree is 26 / 8 = 3.25. Summing all of A_full gives 26, but that's misleading because the graph is undirected, so every link shows up twice. For example, Aridia–Borland is a 1 in Aridia's row and again in Borland's row. Dividing by 2 gives the actual 13 unique edges.

**GQ2.** First, trading partners affect each other's economies. If a country's partners are growing, they buy more of its exports, and if its partners have high inflation, some of that can carry over through the prices of what it imports. So a country's own five numbers only show where it is right now, while its partners hint at what it's exposed to. Second, countries that trade together tend to look alike in our data. Aridia, Borland, Cyrenia, and Galenia mostly trade with each other and all have high growth, exports, and FDI, while Deltora, Estovia, and Hesperia trade with each other and all have high inflation and unemployment. So knowing who a country trades with gives extra evidence about what kind of economy it is, which is what was discussed in class, that the whole point of a graph is that the features are related to other points in the dataset. Since the data is synthetic, these are just illustrations and not real economic claims.

**GQ3.** The five features are on very different scales. Exports go from 27 to 51 (std of about 8.8), while every other feature stays roughly between 2.7 and 8.1 (std of around 1.3 to 1.7). If we used the raw numbers, exports would dominate the Linear layers, the neighbor averages, and the dot products in the decoder just because its numbers are bigger, not because it matters more. Standardizing with `(X - X_mean) / X_std` centers each feature at 0 with a standard deviation of 1, so all five end up on the same scale, which is what was discussed in class about reducing feature dominance and improving optimization. We also kept X_mean and X_std so that in Part D we can scale the shocked Borland data exactly the same way as the original.

## Part B: Building and Training the Graph Autoencoder

**GQ4.** If we only did `A @ H`, each country's new row would just be the sum of its neighbors' rows, so its own features would be left out completely and it would only be described by who it trades with. Adding the identity matrix (`A_hat = A + I`) puts a 1 on the diagonal, so each country also counts itself. Then `D^-1` divides each row by its degree in A_hat, which turns that sum into an average. Without it, Freland (5 partners, so 6 rows added together) would end up with much bigger numbers than Estovia (2 partners, 3 rows) just because it has more neighbors. So one aggregation step gives each country the average of its own features and its direct neighbors' features. To check this on the raw X, Aridia in A_train gets averaged with Borland and Cyrenia, so its inflation becomes (3.1 + 3.8 + 2.7) / 3 = 3.2.

**GQ5.** After one layer, a country only gets information from its direct neighbors (1 hop), since aggregation only mixes rows that are connected in A_hat. In the second layer, those neighbors' rows already contain their own neighbors' information from layer 1, so information can now come from up to 2 hops away. A two-hop path from Freland to Aridia in A_train is Freland → Borland → Aridia, and Freland → Cyrenia → Aridia also works. It can't go through Galenia, because Aridia–Galenia is one of the links we removed. Because Freland connects both groups, two layers are enough for it to reach all 8 countries, while Estovia only reaches 4 (itself, Deltora, Hesperia, and Freland).

**GQ6.** A row of X only has that country's own five numbers, so it says nothing about who the country trades with. The embedding Z comes out of two rounds of aggregation over A_train, so it already has the features of neighbors up to two hops away mixed in. On top of that, the model was trained to rebuild A_train from Z, so Z also has to capture which countries are connected. For example, Freland's scaled features are all close to 0, so from X alone it just looks like an average country. Its embedding `[0.39, 0.07, 0.14]` also sits between the two groups (the first value is 0.85 to 1.09 for Aridia, Borland, Cyrenia, and Galenia, and -0.72 to -1.46 for Deltora, Estovia, and Hesperia), but now it's partly because it is averaged with partners from both sides, which is information X doesn't have.

## Part C: Pattern Recognition Using the Embeddings

**GQ7.** Aridia–Galenia, one of the real links we hid, scored 0.718, while its non-edge control Aridia–Estovia only scored 0.202. Deltora–Hesperia, the other hidden link, scored 0.677 compared to 0.288 for the non-edge Deltora–Cyrenia. So both real edges scored higher than their controls, and by big gaps of about 0.52 and 0.39. This makes sense to us because Aridia and Galenia both trade with Borland and Cyrenia and have similar indicators, and Deltora and Hesperia both trade with Estovia and Freland, while each control pairs a country with someone on the other side of the graph. Still, these are just scores from `sigmoid(Z[i] · Z[j])` and not calibrated probabilities, so 0.718 doesn't mean there's a 72% chance Aridia and Galenia actually trade.

**GQ8.** The top five candidate missing edges were Borland–Cyrenia (0.719), Aridia–Galenia (0.718), Deltora–Hesperia (0.677), Aridia–Freland (0.568), and Estovia–Freland (0.369). Out of the 17 pairs that aren't linked in A_train, Aridia–Galenia came in at rank 2 and Deltora–Hesperia at rank 3. So both would have been discovered near the top, even though BCE was pushing their scores down during training. The only pair above them is Borland–Cyrenia, which isn't a real link in A_full. It makes sense that the model likes it, though, since both countries are in the high-growth, high-export group and they trade with the exact same three partners (Aridia, Freland, and Galenia). For ties, we added the pairs in index order and used Python's stable sort, so a tied pair with the smaller index would stay ahead.

**GQ9.** It's both, but the network clearly matters. If we only looked at the five indicators, Aridia and Galenia would be each other's closest match, since their numbers are almost the same (GDP growth 5.8 vs 5.6, inflation 3.1 vs 3.0, unemployment 4.2 vs 4.5, exports 46 vs 48, FDI 6.2 vs 6.5). But in the embeddings, Aridia's nearest neighbor is Cyrenia (0.2177) and Galenia's is Borland (0.1736). The link between Aridia and Galenia was removed from A_train, so they no longer average in each other's features, while Aridia still averages with Cyrenia and Galenia still averages with Borland. Borland and Cyrenia are also each other's nearest neighbors (0.1168) even though they don't trade, because they share the exact same three partners, so after two rounds of aggregation their embeddings come out almost the same. The features still matter, since every country except Freland, which sits between the groups, has its nearest neighbor inside its own group, but which country in that group ends up closest depends on the trade links.

**GQ10.** Estovia has the largest centroid-distance anomaly score at 1.6319, with Deltora second at 1.0610. Looking at the original indicators, Estovia is the most extreme country on all five: it has the lowest GDP growth (2.8), the highest inflation (7.2), the highest unemployment (8.1), and the lowest exports (27) and FDI (2.8). Its only neighbors are Deltora and Hesperia, which are also in the high-inflation, high-unemployment group, so aggregation doesn't pull it toward the middle the way Freland gets pulled by partners from both sides (Freland ends up closest to the centroid at 0.2721). One limitation is that the centroid is just the average of these eight embeddings, and since four countries are in the high-growth group and only three are in the other, the centroid leans toward the high-growth side, so countries in the smaller group look more unusual partly because of group size. It also only shows how the model placed Estovia after training on this one objective, so it doesn't prove that Estovia's economy is actually abnormal.

## Part D: Shock Propagation and Baseline Comparison

**GQ11.** After changing only Borland's inflation from 3.8 to 12.0 and running the trained encoder again without retraining, Borland's embedding moved the most at 4.3650, followed by Galenia (3.2940), Aridia (2.8691), and Freland (2.1960), while Estovia didn't move at all (0.0000). Borland changed the most since the shock is in its own features, and the next three are all its direct trading partners in A_train, so they average in Borland's new inflation right in the first layer. Freland moved the least out of the three, which we think is because it has 5 partners, so Borland is only 1 of the 6 rows it averages and the change gets diluted. Neighbor count isn't the whole story though, since Galenia moved more than Aridia even though Borland is a bigger share of Aridia's average (1/3 vs 1/4), so the learned weights and ReLU also affect how much each country moves. Non-neighbors can still change because there are two layers. In the second layer, a country averages in its neighbors' rows, which already have Borland's change from layer one, so anything up to two hops away gets affected, like Cyrenia through Aridia, Freland, or Galenia. Estovia is three hops away (Borland → Freland → Deltora → Estovia), so the change never reaches it in a two-layer GNN. This only shows how information moves through our model's network, not how inflation would actually spread between real countries.

**GQ12.** X tells us what each country is like through its five indicators, but nothing about who it trades with. A is the opposite, since it only says who trades with whom using 1s and 0s and says nothing about the countries themselves. The GNN uses both: each layer averages a country's features with its partners' using A_train, so after two layers each embedding has information from countries up to two hops away, and since it's trained to rebuild A_train, the embeddings also have to reflect the network structure. The feature-only baseline (`Linear(5, 8) → ReLU → Linear(8, 3)`) only gets X_scaled, so each country's embedding is built from its own five numbers and nothing else. For example, if we passed the Borland shock from D1 into the baseline, only Borland's embedding would change, while in the GNN it also moved Galenia, Aridia, and Freland. Our baseline is also untrained, so its weights are still random and its embeddings aren't meaningful yet. To actually compare the two, we would need to train both on the same objective (`sigmoid(ZZᵀ)` with BCE on A_train) and then compare things like the final loss and the ranks of the withheld links.

## Evaluation, Limitations, and Findings

**GQ13.** In A_train, the two withheld links Aridia–Galenia and Deltora–Hesperia are set to 0, and since BCE compares the model's scores to A_train, it treats both as non-links. That means every epoch the model gets penalized for giving them a high score, so it is actively being taught that these countries don't trade. That's why it isn't a clean held-out test. In a clean one, the hidden links would be left out of the loss entirely so the model is never told anything about them, but here their scores get pushed down, which makes the test harder than it should be. Even so, after 2000 epochs the model still scored Aridia–Galenia at 0.72 and Deltora–Hesperia at 0.68. One way to fix this, which the handout mentions as an option, is to mask both directions of each withheld link out of the BCE loss, so (0,6), (6,0), (3,7), and (7,3).

**GQ14.** First, the model was only trained to rebuild A_train, which is one snapshot of the network, so it learns which links fit the current pattern and has no idea how trade changes over time. As discussed in class, the adjacency matrix at time t can look different at t+1, and we only ever gave the model one version of it. Second, the scores aren't calibrated probabilities. They're just `sigmoid(Z[i] · Z[j])`, and you can see this in the diagonal, where every country scores at least 0.5 with itself even though that doesn't mean anything. Third, the data is synthetic, with only 8 fictional countries and 5 indicators, and it leaves out things that actually drive trade, like distance, trade agreements, or politics. Lastly, the scores depend a lot on modeling choices. Just switching to symmetric normalization in Part E dropped Aridia–Galenia from 0.718 to 0.577 and swapped the ranks of the two hidden links. Borland–Cyrenia also got the top candidate score (0.719) even though it isn't a real link, which shows that a high score only means two countries look alike to the model.

**GQ15.** One limitation is how small the evaluation is. We only withheld two links, used one random seed, and those two links were still in the BCE target as 0s, so it's hard to tell if ranks 2 and 3 are really good or just lucky. Part E already shows how sensitive it is, since a small change to the aggregation swapped the two ranks. This lines up with what was discussed in class, that this kind of toy setup has no proper train/validation/test edge split or held-out metrics. One way to address it is to hold out more links across several different random splits, leave the held-out pairs out of the loss, and then report the average rank across all the runs instead of relying on one result.

**GQ16.** Our most important finding is that the GNN ranked both withheld real links near the top even though it never saw them as links, since they were removed from the encoder graph and BCE penalized high scores at their positions. Out of the 17 pairs with no link in A_train, Aridia–Galenia ranked 2nd (0.718) and Deltora–Hesperia ranked 3rd (0.677), and both scored well above their non-edge controls (0.202 and 0.288). We think this worked because each hidden pair shares trading partners and has similar indicators, so two rounds of aggregation pulled their embeddings close together. For the optional extension, symmetric normalization didn't really improve the hidden-edge ranking. It got a lower final loss (0.5815 vs 0.5949), but the two hidden links just swapped places, with Deltora–Hesperia moving up to rank 2 (0.708) and Aridia–Galenia dropping to rank 3 (0.577). The nearest neighbors stayed the same except for Freland, whose closest country went from Borland (0.6252) to Galenia (0.7921). Estovia was still the most anomalous in both models (1.6319 vs 1.6400), but the rest of the ordering shifted, with Aridia jumping from 6th to 2nd and Freland going from closest to the centroid (8th) to 6th. So a lower reconstruction loss doesn't automatically mean the model is better at finding missing links, and it can also reshuffle the other results. That's because the loss is measured against A_train, where both hidden links are 0s, so part of a lower loss can come from fitting those 0s better, which is exactly what pushes a hidden link's score down (like Aridia–Galenia falling from 0.718 to 0.577). The loss is also averaged over all 64 entries, so it measures how well the model rebuilds the training graph overall, not how it ranks the two pairs we actually care about.
